# Question bank patterns

How to write the four to six questions per slot. `[n]` numbers follow the
recruiting dossier (section 8).

## 1. Behavioural (past behaviour) [27][28]

"Tell me about a time you <did the job behaviour>." Best for complex or
managerial roles [27]. Ask for one real instance, then probe. Example: "Walk
me through a billing or payment bug you traced to its root."

## 2. Situational (what would you do) [27]

A realistic job scenario with a decision in it: "A customer was charged
twice. Where do you look first, and why?" Situational questions also
predict [27]; keep them concrete and job-shaped, never "if you were an
animal". A "fortune-teller" hypothetical about the distant future is an
anti-pattern [23].

## 3. Most Significant Accomplishment [26]

"What's the accomplishment you're proudest of that's most like <the role's
first-year outcome>?" Then probe with SMARTe follow-ups: specific task,
measurable result, action taken, results, time frame, environment [26].

## 4. Chronological and "what / how / tell me more" [22][24]

For a career walk-through (screen or hiring-manager slot): for each role,
what were you hired to do, what did you accomplish, what went wrong, who did
you work with. Follow each answer with "What? How? Tell me more." [24]. Use
Topgrading-style per-role questions without its adversarial framing [25].

## 5. Job-related problem [26]

A problem the role actually faces, solved together. Give it a clock; it
overruns and eats the candidate's time (skill guidance). For take-homes, say
how long it should take and what AI use is allowed (`ai-affect-and-candidate-ai.md`).

## 6. Probes that test ownership

Under every core question, one probe that tests whether the candidate did
the work themselves:
- "Which part was yours?"
- "What number moved?"
- "What would you change?"
- "What did you log so you'd know next time?"
- "Who disagreed, and what did you do?"

## 7. Drop rules (skill; lint warns on most)

Drop any question that:
- checks recognition of a tool name rather than use of it ("Have you used Kafka?");
- signals the wanted answer ("You're fine with ambiguity, right?");
- bundles two questions into one;
- a candidate could answer by reading the posting back;
- is a trick or brainteaser, or a pet question with no link to a must-have [23].

Tighten and open up the owner's own questions before adding new ones: keep
their intent, fix the form, and show what changed in the kit's "Dropped or
rewritten" table.

## 8. Strong-answer notes

Each question gets one line on what a strong answer shows, in observable
terms (what they did, what they measured, what changed), not traits. Do not
reward polish of speech or writing: LLM-era evaluators over-prefer polished
AI-style text [63], and polish is not the competency. Rate substance against
the anchors.
