## 1. Requirement inflation

Firms "piled on job requirements" and baked them into applicant-tracking
software until virtually no applicants met them all; only about a third of US
companies check whether their hiring produces good employees, and only about
40% of employers test skills at all [36]. Every extra must-have shrinks the pool
without evidence that it predicts the work. Hence three to six must-haves, and
the question "what fails without it?" for each.

## 2. Skills-based hiring "in name only"

Across about 11,300 roles at large firms, fewer than 1 in 700 new hires
benefited from removed degree requirements, and roughly 45% of firms changed in
name only [111]. Removing "degree required" from a posting changes nothing if
the screen and the loop still look for it. Rule: every must-have names the
evidence method that will test it, and `interview-kit-design` gives each
must-have one owner in the loop.

## 3. The three-part must-have test

A pattern from the federal contractor "basic qualifications" definition:
qualifications that are **objective**, **non-comparative** and **relevant to the
job** [49]. The regulation is being rescinded for contractors effective
2026-10-26 [50]; the three tests remain a sound pattern (practitioner
judgement, unsourced).

| Test | Fails | Passes |
|---|---|---|
| Objective | "Strong communicator" | "Has written design docs other engineers acted on" |
| Non-comparative | "Among the best Go engineers we've met" | "Has run Go services in production" |
| Job-relevant | "Plays chess competitively" | "Has debugged payment flows end to end" (the role owns billing) |

## 4. Outcomes, not pedigree

A scorecard sets out the mission and a short list of measurable outcomes before
any sourcing starts [22]; performance objectives replace skills lists [26].
Years of education predict job performance weakly (about .10 in the older
canon) [18]. Keep a years or degree floor only when the HM defends it in one
line; record the defence verbatim so the same test is applied to everyone.

## 5. Location as work terms

Illinois HB 3773 names the use of ZIP codes as a proxy for protected classes
[72]. Write "on-site in <city> three days a week" or "overlaps 10:00-15:00
CET", never a postcode, commute radius or "local candidates only".

## 6. Referral-only slates

Referrals can account for a large share of hires, but their performance
advantage fades if the referrer leaves [36], and the EEOC lists word-of-mouth
recruiting from a homogeneous workforce as a practice that can discriminate
[34]. If the owner plans a referral-only slate, say so once and let them decide.
